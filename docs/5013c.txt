# TIRO Foundation — Node Zero to Proposed U.S. 501(c)(3)

Status: Advisory institutional strategy and proposed governance; not adopted charter, bylaws, or filing.
Revision date: 2026-10-02.
Owner: TIRO founder / Steward; future board owns adoption of governing instruments.
Authority: Planning recommendations derived from the public manifesto and stewardship direction. These files do not establish incorporation, IRS recognition, tax deductibility, director appointments, or operational preservation readiness.
Supersession: Revises the September 2026 advisory memo in this file. Historical reasoning in §1 remains context; §2 distinguishes current direction from proposals and verified status. This document does not supersede adopted law, governing instruments, or applicable source permissions.
Related sources: `docs/manifestov0-2.txt` is the public mission baseline; `/community-rights` (`src/app/community-rights/page.tsx`) states stewardship principles; `docs/ethos.txt` supplies proposed operational acceptance criteria and lifecycle procedures; `docs/memory.txt` records prototype history.

This memo reconstructs the institutional direction and proposes a path to incorporation. Recommendations become institutional authority only through the relevant adoption or filing, with dated evidence.

The central conclusion first: the organization that should become the U.S. 501(c)(3) is the institutional TIRO: a durable custodian of cultural and linguistic memory, with particular attention to endangered and under-documented material. Neutrality means non-appropriation and fair handling of contested accounts, not avoidance of accountable curation. Node Zero is its founding web portal and institutional prototype, becoming an operational custodian only after the readiness gates.
 
That distinction gives us a clean line from where we are now to incorporation.
 
1. TIRO, reconstructed
 
There have effectively been four TIROs, but they are not four separate projects.
 
TIRO as philosophy
 
The oldest and most persistent layer is philosophical.
 
The foundational sentence became:
 
> “There is enlightenment in exploration, but empathy is essential.”
 
 
 
That idea has survived essentially every incarnation of TIRO. Exploration matters, but the means of exploration have an ethical constraint: the human beings, communities, histories and identities being encountered cannot simply become raw material.
 
Over time this hardened into what I would call the TIRO institutional temperament:
 
curiosity without extraction;
 
preservation without appropriation;
 
access without dispossession;
 
technology without techno-solutionism;
 
permanence over virality;
 
stewardship rather than ownership;
 
infrastructure rather than empire.
 
 
The role title you eventually settled on—“Steward, TIRO”—fits this remarkably well.
 
TIRO Foundation
 
This became the institutional form.
 
At various points we described TIRO as an organization concerned with disappearing languages, oral history, cultural preservation and intergenerational memory. A bounded Igbo oral-memory pilot in Nigeria became the proposed first proving ground, subject to community scoping and authorization. Nigeria is an initial geographic focus rather than a permanent jurisdictional boundary. This is not a claim that every Igbo variety or cultural practice is endangered.
 
Pointe-Noire, Republic of the Congo also appeared in the Foundation's geographic imagination, particularly as TIRO evolved beyond being implicitly Nigerian and toward something pan-African/global.
 
The enduring charitable thesis became roughly:
 
Human knowledge exists that may disappear because it is inadequately recorded, inadequately transmitted or insufficiently protected. TIRO exists to preserve that knowledge with the people to whom it belongs.
 
That eventually gave rise to the Memory Project.
 
The Memory Project
 
This is the strongest candidate for TIRO's first formal charitable program.
 
The idea developed from a fairly conventional digital archive into something considerably more interesting.
 
The early conception included:
 
recording elders and native speakers;
 
oral histories;
 
endangered and under-documented languages;
 
stories, proverbs, songs, customs and local histories;
 
audio, video, transcripts and translations;
 
geographical and cultural metadata;
 
community-contributed material;
 
interactive maps;
 
thematic journeys;
 
eventually education and language-learning material made by native speakers rather than generated from outside the culture.
 
 
The interactive globe/map became particularly important because it changes the archive from “a database of dying things” into an explorable representation of living human memory.
 
The first serious implementation logic was essentially:
 
Igbo → Nigeria → additional communities → distributed global archive.
 
Importantly, we also moved away from “TIRO owns a bunch of cultural data.”
 
The stronger doctrine became: communities remain the intellectual and cultural principals; TIRO is the custodian.
 
That distinction is foundational.
 
The Custodian Doctrine
 
This may be the most consequential conceptual work we have done on TIRO.
 
The analogy we used was deliberately odd but extremely useful: BNY Mellon.
 
Not because TIRO should resemble a bank commercially, but because custody infrastructure has several properties TIRO needs:
 
it can be enormously consequential without owning the underlying asset;
 
trust matters more than visibility;
 
integrity matters more than engagement;
 
provenance matters;
 
redundancy matters;
 
records must survive changes in personnel;
 
institutional counterparties matter;
 
custodial infrastructure should not determine the value or meaning of the thing entrusted to it.
 
 
That led to “silent indispensability” as an institutional archetype.
 
TIRO does not need to become the world's loudest cultural organization.
 
It should aspire to become one of the systems that historians, communities, researchers, archives and cultural institutions quietly assume will still exist fifty years from now.
 
The eventual network we imagined included counterparties such as universities, national archives, cultural ministries, language commissions, UNESCO-linked institutions, indigenous/community councils and local archival organizations.
 
The strongest formulation was effectively:
 
TIRO is not the owner of the world's memory. TIRO is part of the infrastructure that ensures memory cannot easily disappear.
 
That is 501(c)(3)-grade material.
 
Governance: Steward-Protector
 
We developed an unusually deliberate founder-governance model because TIRO's mission creates a genuine continuity problem.
 
A normal founder-controlled organization is dangerous.
 
A normal board-controlled organization can also be dangerous if a future board decides that TIRO should become a commercial AI company, sell its archive, abandon cultural sovereignty, or transform itself into something antithetical to its founding purpose.
 
The mechanism we explored was the Steward-Protector / Founder-Protector model.
 
The key concept was not unlimited veto power.
 
It was mission protection.
 
The version developed in our discussions included something close to:
 
Founder-Protector objection → 90-day pause → independent review → board reconsideration → proposed 75% supermajority override. The detailed proposed scope, calculation, deadlines, and succession are in §6 Stage 5; this historical summary does not itself confer authority.
 
The intent was therefore “constitutional brake,” not “founder monarch.”
 
That distinction will become extremely important when we draft the actual nonprofit governance documents.
 
The IRS requires a 501(c)(3) to serve public rather than private interests and prohibits earnings from inuring to founders or other insiders. 
 
So I would preserve the Steward-Protector doctrine but modify its legal implementation.
 
More on that below.
 
TIRO AI
 
There was also an entirely different technological lineage.
 
TIRO became, for a period, the name of a local-first personal intelligence system.
 
That version explored:
 
conversational AI;
 
persistent memory;
 
journaling;
 
local execution;
 
voice;
 
context awareness;
 
modular “nodes”;
 
dynamic mini-app creation;
 
personal data sovereignty;
 
local-first architecture;
 
different reasoning/personality modes;
 
a distributed “Node's Journey” architecture.
 
 
There were directories and concepts such as core, nodes, memory, foundation, shadow, etc., and an early CLI/node architecture.
 
This eventually intersected with TIRO Foundation conceptually because AI could help people navigate memory, but it should not be allowed to define the nonprofit.
 
I would now classify this material as:
 
TIRO technology R&D, not TIRO's charitable identity.
 
Some of it will probably reappear.
 
For instance, a culturally literate archival AI that can explore the Memory Project while respecting provenance and permissions is entirely consistent with TIRO.
 
A generic personal assistant that builds mini-apps for the user is not necessarily a charitable activity.
 
That project could someday become:
 
TIRO Labs, a separate subsidiary, open-source project, research program or even separate company.
 
But I would keep it outside the initial 501(c)(3) application.
 
The Witness
 
“The Witness” emerged later as a better metaphor for the AI's role inside the Foundation.
 
Instead of an AI that speaks with authority about cultures, it becomes an interface that helps people encounter recorded testimony, provenance and contextual material.
 
That is much closer to the Custodian Doctrine.
 
The AI is not:
 
“Here is what Igbo culture means.”
 
It is closer to:
 
“Here are the people, recordings, sources and contexts from which this information comes.”
 
That is an extraordinarily important design principle.
 
The Witness therefore belongs in TIRO's long-term architecture, but probably not as an initial program promise in the IRS filing.

3. The 501(c)(3) TIRO should become
 
I recommend:
 
Proposed target: TIRO Foundation incorporated as a Texas nonprofit corporation, applying for IRS recognition under 501(c)(3) and an appropriate public-charity classification. Neither incorporation nor recognition is claimed as completed.
 
Not a private foundation.
 
Not Delaware by default.
 
Not an LLC.
 
Not a fiscal-sponsorship-only permanent structure.
 
Texas Form 202 lists a $25 filing fee and at least three initial directors for a board-managed nonprofit as reviewed on 2026-10-02 [S4]. Check current requirements and fees again before filing.
 
Before fundraising, assess applicable solicitation requirements in Texas and every jurisdiction where TIRO will seek support; incorporation and federal recognition do not settle those obligations.
 
Given that TIRO is presently rooted here and its activities will be international, there isn't an obvious governance advantage that justifies incorporating it elsewhere at this stage.
 
The organizing document should limit TIRO to qualifying exempt purposes under section 501(c)(3), initially educational and charitable purposes. Cultural preservation is an activity advancing those purposes, not a separate statutory exempt-purpose category. Include scientific purposes only if the adopted research program and operations genuinely support that framing.
 
The certificate itself should be narrower and cleaner than our philosophical literature.
 
Proposed purpose-clause concept, for review in the incorporation packet:

> TIRO Foundation is organized exclusively for educational and charitable purposes within the meaning of section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code. In furtherance of those purposes, it preserves, documents, studies, and supports responsible access to cultural and linguistic memory, particularly endangered, under-documented, and orally transmitted knowledge; educates the public through contextual resources and programming; and strengthens community preservation capacity.

The public program mission remains broader in expression than this organizing clause, but all corporate activities must further the qualifying purposes. Curation follows community priorities, risk of loss, documentation gaps, cultural significance, and the ability to honor permissions and preservation duties (`docs/manifestov0-2.txt` II).
 
 
 
That is not yet the final legal language—I would draft that against IRS organizational-test requirements before filing—but it is the right conceptual territory.
 
The organizing document must limit TIRO to qualifying exempt purposes and permanently dedicate TIRO-owned assets to exempt purposes upon dissolution [S1]. Net earnings may not inure to insiders, and operations must serve public rather than private interests [S2]. These IRS requirements are distinct from TIRO's optional Protector design.

**Owned charitable assets versus entrusted material:** separately inventory TIRO-owned funds, equipment, software, and any rights actually acquired, versus recordings, documents, metadata, or other material held under custody agreements or licenses. Custody does not transfer ownership; restrictions follow the actual rights and agreements, even when stored on TIRO equipment.

On merger, transfer, or dissolution, owned net charitable assets remain dedicated to qualifying exempt/public purposes. Entrusted material may be transferred only under applicable source/legal authorization and compatible custody terms carrying provenance, access restrictions, retention limits, and withdrawal duties. Identify and notify the relevant authorities; seek any required fresh authorization. If lawful successor custody is unavailable, arrange authorized return or deletion, subject to applicable retention duties. Do not characterize the entire corpus as freely disposable TIRO property or promise donors ownership of it.

4. I would use the full Form 1023
 
TIRO might initially qualify for the streamlined Form 1023-EZ if projected annual gross receipts stay at or below $50,000 and assets at or below $250,000, assuming it passes the rest of the eligibility worksheet. 
 
I nevertheless think 1023-EZ is the wrong instrument for TIRO.
 
IRS fees reviewed on 2026-10-02 are $275 for 1023-EZ and $600 for the full Form 1023 [S5]; check again at filing.
 
Spend the extra $325.
 
Why?
 
Because TIRO will have:
 
international operations, intellectual-property questions, cultural-rights policy, archival custody, community contributors, potentially AI, institutional partnerships and an unusual founder-protection governance mechanism.
 
The full Form 1023 forces us to articulate the institution.
 
For some tiny nonprofits, that paperwork is a burden.
 
For TIRO, it is useful constitutional engineering.

5. Public charity, deliberately
 
The IRS divides 501(c)(3)s primarily into public charities and private foundations; broad public support generally characterizes the former. 
 
TIRO should deliberately build toward public charity status.
 
The natural model is probably §509(a)(1) / §170(b)(1)(A)(vi): support from the public, governments and other public charities.
 
The conventional test is at least one-third qualifying public support, with an alternative 10%-plus facts-and-circumstances route. 
 
Which means something subtle:
 
we should avoid making issimo TIRO's permanent financial life-support machine.
 
Founder seed funding is fine.
 
Founder financial dependency is not the institution we want anyway.
 
Diversified support becomes both a tax characteristic and proof that TIRO has escaped founder gravity.

Proposed governance design for adoption in the certificate/bylaws, with legal review for enforceability and fiduciary duties:

| Body / capacity | Authority and limits | Accountability / review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fiduciary board | Governs charity, budget, appointments, delegations, program policy, risk and owned assets; cannot create permissions over entrusted material | Majority independent as proposed; minutes, conflicts policy, financial oversight and mission duties |
| Steward-Protector | May trigger a time-limited mission-protection pause for the enumerated constitutional events; no general operational veto | Written reasons, independent review, fixed deadlines, board override, non-hereditary succession |
| Ethics committee / ethics reviewers | Delegated sensitivity review, protective holds, cultural-protocol advice and first independent handling appeal; not a second fiduciary board | Board-approved charter, named non-conflicted reviewers and community participation; cannot broaden consent |
| Regional / collection stewards | Execute approved protocols, capacity checks, access decisions within grants, withdrawal and incident response | Written delegation, training, readiness evidence, reports and escalation to ethics/board |
| Source authorities, legal rights holders, subjects and authorized representatives | Retain the applicable cultural, legal, privacy and consent interests identified for each use | Shared/disputed claims documented; TIRO review can decide its own handling but cannot extinguish outside authority |

**Pause, independent review, and override (proposal):**

1. For a qualifying event, the Protector delivers a reasoned written objection within 14 calendar days of formal notice of the proposed decision. A timely objection pauses that decision for at most 90 calendar days from receipt; no rolling or repeat pause on the same materially unchanged proposal. An unavailable or vacant Protector does not create an automatic stay.
2. Within 15 days of objection, non-conflicted directors appoint three independent reviewers with nonprofit-governance, preservation, and community-rights competence, including a community-selected nominee accepted for independence. Reviewers may not be the Protector, current directors, close family, financially dependent persons, or beneficiaries of the transaction. The board maintains a vetted reserve panel to prevent appointment deadlock; the Protector does not appoint or control it.
3. Reviewers receive relevant safe evidence and affected-community submissions and issue a reasoned report by day 60. The board reconsiders by day 90 with reasons and a record of conflicts. If review is delayed, request the reserve panel and disclose the gap; the stay still expires at day 90, and proceeding without a report requires the same override threshold and a documented explanation.
4. Override requires affirmative votes of at least `ceil(0.75 × N)`, where N is the number of seated, non-conflicted directors eligible on that matter at the formal notice date. Abstentions and absences remain in N; conflicted directors and already-vacant seats do not. Resignation does not reduce the fixed denominator; replacements fill the relevant eligible seats. This protects against manipulating the vote during the stay.
5. At least three non-conflicted directors and the applicable bylaw/statutory quorum must participate. If fewer can act, restore an independent eligible board before discretionary action. Expiry releases the temporary stay, not the approval requirement: the objected proposal proceeds only after the required recorded approval or withdrawal of the objection. Ordinary urgent safeguarding may continue under documented board authority; this proposal cannot delay legally required protective action.
6. An override changes only TIRO's internal decision. It never authorizes a source-rights violation, expands consent, or defeats applicable law/custody terms. An unlawful transfer is prohibited independently of the Protector.

**Succession and vacancy (proposal):** the founder may be the initial Protector only by express adoption and appointment. The office is not inheritable, saleable, or transferable by private designation. Subsequent Protectors serve five-year terms, with at most one renewal. Non-conflicted ethics/community representatives nominate qualified independent candidates; the board appoints by 75% of all seated non-conflicted directors, rounded up, with at least three participating and a lawful quorum. The founder may recommend a successor but cannot appoint one unilaterally.

On death, resignation, incapacity, expiry, removal, or vacancy, the board records the event, opens nominations within 30 days and aims to appoint within 90 days. Pending appointment, a designated non-conflicted ethics panel may trigger the same limited pause without acquiring general Protector or fiduciary authority. Incapacity, misconduct, and removal require documented evidence, an opportunity to respond where feasible, independent review, and the same 75% board threshold. Review of the Protector's own appointment, compensation, conflicts, or removal cannot be vetoed by the Protector. Long-term vacancies do not prevent ordinary board governance; reasons and continuing recruitment are reported.

**Delegation, handling appeals, and conflicts (proposal):** publish contacts and scope; name primary and backup stewards before collection. Anyone may raise a concern. Non-conflicted ethics reviewers acknowledge an appeal within 2 calendar days and issue a decision or status within 30; unresolved appeals go to non-conflicted directors for a reasoned response within a further 30 days. Protective restrictions remain during disputed authority; appeal decisions do not restore access without applicable authorization. Recusal, evidence access, deadlines, decisions, and further applicable external remedies are documented. Product action windows are in `docs/ethos.txt` §12.

These percentages, terms, and deadlines are TIRO governance choices proposed for review, not IRS mandates. The legal requirement against private benefit/inurement [S2] applies independently. Board fiduciary responsibility remains with the board; mission protection is bounded, reviewable, and separable from the founder.
